Celiac Disease Basics

Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition in which eating gluten damages the lining of the small intestine. It is not a food allergy and not a lifestyle choice: it is a serious medical condition that affects about one person in a hundred, although many cases go undiagnosed. The only treatment is a strict gluten-free diet for life. This guide explains what celiac disease is, how it is diagnosed, why the diet must be strict, and how to live well with the condition.

What celiac disease is

In celiac disease, the immune system reacts to gluten, a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye, and attacks the small intestine. The tiny finger-like structures that absorb nutrients, called villi, become flattened and damaged, so the body cannot absorb vitamins and minerals properly. Symptoms vary widely: some people have diarrhoea, bloating, and weight loss, while others have tiredness, anaemia, skin rashes, or no obvious symptoms at all. Untreated celiac disease can lead to osteoporosis, infertility, and other complications, so diagnosis and treatment matter.

How it is diagnosed

Celiac disease is diagnosed with a blood test that looks for specific antibodies, followed by a biopsy of the small intestine to confirm the damage. It is important to keep eating gluten until all tests are complete, because the blood test and the biopsy only work if gluten is in the diet. Do not start a gluten-free diet before testing, or the results may be wrong. If the blood test is positive, your doctor will refer you to a gastroenterologist for the biopsy. Genetic testing can also help, because almost everyone with celiac disease carries one of two genes, although many people with those genes never develop the condition.

Why the diet must be strict

Even small amounts of gluten can damage the intestine of a person with celiac disease, even if they feel no symptoms. A crumb of bread, a shared toaster, or a fryer used for breaded food can be enough to cause damage. That is why the diet must be strict, not just low in gluten. It also means reading labels, asking about cross-contact in restaurants, and being careful at home with shared equipment. The good news is that the intestine heals on a strict gluten-free diet, usually within months, and most people feel much better within weeks.

Living well with celiac disease

Living with celiac disease gets easier with practice. Learn to read labels, cook at home more often, and keep a stock of gluten-free basics so you are never caught out. Join a celiac support group, online or in person, for recipes, restaurant tips, and reassurance. Tell friends and family what you need, and do not be embarrassed to ask questions in restaurants. Keep up with follow-up appointments and blood tests, and ask your doctor about vitamin supplements if needed. With a strict diet, most people with celiac disease live full, healthy lives.

Telling friends and family

Telling friends and family about celiac disease is an important step, and most people are understanding once they know what it means. Explain it simply: gluten damages your intestine, even in small amounts, so you need to avoid it completely. Tell them what you can eat and what you cannot, and offer to bring a dish to shared meals so there is always something safe for you. Most people want to help but do not know how, so clear, practical guidance is welcome. With time, your friends and family will learn the rules, and eating together will become easy again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is celiac disease the same as a wheat allergy?

No. Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition that damages the intestine. A wheat allergy is an immune reaction that can cause symptoms within minutes, and it is different from celiac disease.

Can celiac disease be cured?

No, there is no cure yet. The only treatment is a strict gluten-free diet for life, which allows the intestine to heal.

Do I need to eat gluten before being tested?

Yes. Keep eating gluten until all tests are complete, or the blood test and biopsy may give a false negative result.

Important: if you have celiac disease or a wheat allergy, always check labels and look for certified gluten-free products. This site is general information only — talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing your diet.
